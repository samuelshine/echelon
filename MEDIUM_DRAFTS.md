# Building Echelon — Field Notes from the Frontend

_A running Medium draft documenting the UX and architecture decisions behind the console for an AI security firewall._

---

## Article 1 — Designing UX for AI Security Tools: When Your Users Are Two People at Once

Most dashboards are built for one person. You imagine them, you name them, you design every pixel around their goals. The console for Echelon — an ultra-low-latency firewall that sits in the critical path between an application and its LLM — doesn't get that luxury. It has to serve two people who almost never agree on what "good UI" means.

The first is a **Product Manager**. They open the dashboard on a Monday morning and want a single, honest answer: *is our AI healthy, safe, and on-budget?* They don't want to know what a DistilBERT classifier is. They want a number that went up or down, a line that's trending the wrong way, and a cost figure they can take into a meeting. For them, density is the enemy and clarity is everything. The second is a **Security Engineer**. They arrive at 2 a.m. because an alert fired, and they want the *opposite* of clarity-through-simplification. They want the raw prompt, the exact layer that tripped, the risk score to three decimal places, and the JSON payload that proves it. Hide that behind friendly abstractions and you've built them a toy.

The temptation is to build two products. The better answer — and the thesis of this whole build — is **progressive disclosure**: one surface that opens layer by layer. The PM lives on the top layer and never has to leave it. The engineer clicks, expands, and drills until they hit raw bytes. The same "why was this blocked?" question resolves to a single sentence for one user and a full cascade trace for the other. Getting that gradient right — glanceable at the top, forensic at the bottom, with no hard wall between them — is the central UX problem of this project, and nearly every design decision downstream is really a decision about *where on that gradient* a given piece of information belongs.

There's a second, subtler constraint that shapes everything: **latency is the product**. Echelon's entire pitch is that it adds only microseconds of overhead to an LLM call. A console that feels sluggish would quietly contradict the thing it's selling. So "fast" here isn't a nice-to-have — it's brand-critical. That pushes us toward virtualized tables over paginated ones, skeletons over spinners, optimistic UI on config changes, and a hard budget on interaction latency. In the next entries I'll get concrete about the stack that lets us hit those numbers, and about the one component I'm most nervous about: a bespoke visualizer for the three-fold risk cascade that no charting library ships out of the box.

## Article 2 — Choosing a Palette for a Product Whose Pitch Is Speed

When I sat down to give Echelon a visual identity, I had one non-obvious constraint pulling against every default I'd normally reach for. This is a security product, and the reflex for security tooling is to go dark, high-contrast, and vaguely menacing — the "SOC at 2 a.m." aesthetic. But the stakeholder who signs off on Echelon isn't only the engineer watching a live log; it's also a Product Manager who wants to screenshot a number into a slide deck. So the brief that came back was *classical, modern, minimalist* — three words that quietly rule out the neon-on-black cliché. The interesting design problem became: how do you make something feel authoritative and precise *without* leaning on the visual language of a hacker movie?

The answer I committed to is a system I'm calling **"The Assay Ledger."** The metaphor is a classical assay office — the institution that tests metal and stamps a verdict of purity on it — because that is, almost exactly, what Echelon does to a prompt: it runs it through three tests and stamps a verdict. That framing did real work. It pushed me toward a *cool* off-white paper rather than the warm cream that AI design tools reach for by default, toward hairline rules and engraved restraint instead of glow and gradients, and toward a deep-petrol brand color that reads institutional rather than alarming. Crucially, the severity colors — the red for a blocked prompt, the amber for a flag — are deliberately *desaturated*. On a security dashboard, a saturated red is a boy who cries wolf; if everything is urgent, nothing is. Muting the palette means that when a real oxblood-red "block" appears, your eye actually goes to it. Typography does the rest of the talking: an optical serif (Fraunces) for display gives the classical weight, a neutral grotesque (Inter) keeps the body quiet, and a monospace (JetBrains Mono) is reserved exclusively for data — every risk score, latency figure, and raw JSON key. The rule is simple and load-bearing: *if it's a number the user might act on, it's monospace and tabular*, so digits line up and scan like a ledger.

On the architecture side, the headline decision was to treat this as a **server-state problem, not a client-state problem** — which is why there's no Redux here. Almost everything the console shows is data owned by the Echelon backend: logs, metrics, config, keys. That's a caching-and-synchronization job, and it belongs to TanStack Query, not to a hand-rolled global store. The thin sliver of genuinely local state (which panel is open, the live-tail toggle, a filter draft) will live in a couple of small Zustand slices. The second decision that will pay off for months is that I built the entire Phase 0 against a **seeded, deterministic mock backend** validated by the exact same Zod schemas the real API will eventually have to satisfy. This means the whole team can build, screenshot, and demo against stable, realistic data today — the dashboard shows the same numbers on every reload — and the day the real Echelon API ships, we swap one module behind a typed contract and nothing else in the app has to know. Designing against a contract instead of against a live server is the difference between a frontend that's blocked on the backend and one that's merely *ready* for it.

## Article 3 — The Color of an Attack: Validating a Palette Instead of Guessing One

The moment you put six categories of cyberattack into a stacked chart, you inherit a problem most dashboards quietly get wrong: color. The instinct is to open a color picker, choose six hues that "look nice together," and move on. But roughly one in twelve men has some form of color-vision deficiency, and a palette that separates cleanly for me might collapse into two indistinguishable browns for them. On a *security* dashboard, that's not an aesthetic miss — it's an operator failing to tell a prompt-injection spike from a data-exfiltration spike because the two ribbons look identical. So for Echelon I made a rule: the palette is not a taste decision, it's a computed one. Every categorical palette gets run through a validator that checks perceptual lightness bands, a chroma floor, colorblind-safe separation (a ΔE distance under simulated protanopia and tritanopia), and contrast against the actual surface it renders on. If it fails, it doesn't ship — no matter how good it looks to me.

This created a genuine tension with the "classical, minimalist" design language I'd committed to. My brand palette is deliberately *muted* — desaturated petrol, oxblood, ochre — because muting is what makes it feel restrained and institutional. But muted colors are exactly the ones that fail CVD separation and contrast checks; distinguishability lives in saturation and lightness contrast, the very things minimalism wants to turn down. The resolution was to split the system in two. The *chrome* — surfaces, ink, borders, the brand accent — stays quiet and classical. The *data* is allowed to be more saturated than the rest of the UI, because in a chart, distinguishability is a functional requirement, not a decoration. I ran my six attack-category hues through the validator against Echelon's own light and dark surfaces (not the tool's defaults — contrast is only meaningful against the surface you actually render on), and where the validator flagged two slots as sub-contrast on white, I didn't overrule it. I applied the "relief rule" instead: an always-present legend and a 2-pixel gap between every stacked segment, so identity never rides on color alone.

The engineering choice that makes all of this maintainable is that **not a single chart color is hard-coded in a component**. Recharts, like most React charting libraries, wants you to pass hex strings as props — and that's a trap, because it scatters your palette across dozens of files and breaks the moment you add a dark mode. Instead, every series color is a CSS custom property (`--color-viz-prompt_injection`), defined once per theme in the design system, and the chart references the *role*, not the value. SVG happily accepts `fill="var(--color-viz-jailbreak)"`, which means the entire dashboard re-themes for dark mode with zero JavaScript and zero prop changes — the same mechanism that themes the buttons themes the charts. The lesson generalizes past this project: treat your visualization palette as part of your design system's token layer, not as arguments you sprinkle into chart components, and validate it with a script instead of your eyes. Color is the one part of data viz that is genuinely computable — so compute it.

## Article 4 — Progressive Disclosure, Made Concrete: One Drawer for Two Users

Two articles ago I argued that the whole console lives or dies on progressive disclosure — one surface that a Product Manager reads at the top and a Security Engineer drills to the bottom. The Threat Audit log is where that thesis stops being a slogan and becomes a component you can click. When an engineer opens a blocked prompt, the drawer doesn't greet them with a wall of JSON, and it doesn't greet the PM with a friendly euphemism either. It opens in three deliberately stacked layers, and *where you stop reading* is the personalization — no mode switch, no "advanced view" toggle, just depth you opt into by scrolling.

The top layer is a single declarative sentence: **"Blocked at ML Classifier · risk 0.89."** That is the entire answer for 80% of the people who will ever open this panel. Below it sits the middle layer — the cascade trace — which is the most interesting design object in the module. It renders all three stages of the pipeline even when the prompt never reached the third, because *the absence of a verdict is itself information*: a stage greyed out and marked "skipped" tells the engineer the cascade short-circuited, which is how the firewall keeps its latency budget. Each stage shows its score as a bar with a thin vertical tick marking the routing threshold, so "0.89 against a 0.60 threshold" is something you *see* as a bar overshooting a line, not arithmetic you perform in your head. Only at the very bottom, collapsed behind a disclosure triangle, is the raw event JSON — the ground truth, one click away, for the engineer who trusts nothing they didn't read in the payload. Nobody is condescended to and nobody is buried.

The engineering decisions underneath are quieter but matter more for whether this scales. First, the table is **virtualized** from day one: only the ~15 rows in the viewport exist in the DOM at any moment, so the ledger stays at 60fps whether it holds five hundred rows or five million — non-negotiable for a product whose entire pitch is that it doesn't add latency. Second, every filter runs through a single `applyFilters` function that today reads an in-memory array but is shaped exactly like the query object a backend would take; when the real API arrives, filtering and sorting move server-side by swapping that one seam, and not a single component changes. Third — a small thing that signals seriousness — verdict state is never carried by color alone. A "block" is an oxblood pill *with an ✕ glyph and the word "Block,"* because a security tool that fails colorblind operators fails an operator, and on this dashboard a missed distinction is a missed attack. The lesson that keeps recurring in this build: the flashy component (the cascade trace) earns the attention, but the invisible decisions (virtualize early, filter behind a seam, never encode meaning in hue alone) are what make it trustworthy.

## Article 5 — Designing the One Component You'll Be Remembered By

Every product has a component that becomes its signature — the thing a user could sketch from memory after a demo. For a charting tool it's the chart; for Echelon it had to be the moment of judgment: the instant a prompt is run through three tests and stamped. I'd been deferring this component for three phases, using an honest placeholder (a plain list of bars) in its place, because I wanted to build the surfaces *around* it first and understand exactly how it would be used before committing to a form. That patience paid off: by the time I designed it, I knew it had to work at three very different sizes — a glanceable inline strip in a dense table row, a hero element on the dashboard, and a detailed trace in the drill-down drawer — which immediately ruled out anything that only reads at one scale.

The design metaphor came straight from the product's name and world. "Echelon" and "assay" both carry an air of institutional judgment, so I built the component as an **engraved assay certificate**: three stamped segments framed by double hairline rules, each stage showing its score in the serif display face against a threshold tick, connected by a rail. The single best decision — the one that turns a diagram into a *story* — is what the rail does when a prompt is blocked. Up to the blocking stage the rail is a solid 2px line; after it, the rail turns **dashed and faded**, and the deeper stages render as hollow "not reached" nodes. That's not decoration: it's the literal truth of how the firewall works. Echelon protects its latency budget by short-circuiting — the moment a cheap early layer is confident, it stops and never pays for the expensive LLM judge. Most dashboards would communicate that with a sentence in a tooltip. Making it *visible in the geometry* — the path literally stops being drawn — means an engineer understands the system's cost model at a glance, and a PM intuits "we caught it early and cheap" without being told. On top of the blocking segment sits a rotated wax-seal stamp with an inset double border, the letterpress flourish that earns the "certificate" metaphor. Per the design discipline I set myself, that seal is the *one* bold thing; everything around it stays quiet.

The reuse story is where the architecture and the design meet. The same component, driven by a `variant` prop, is the compact node-strip in the table, the full certificate in the drawer, and the showcase on the dashboard — one source of truth for "what does a verdict look like," so the visual language can never drift between screens. There was one genuinely fiddly engineering constraint worth naming: the table row is a `<button>` (the whole row is the click target that opens the drawer), and HTML forbids block-level elements inside a button. The compact variant therefore had to be built entirely from inline `<span>` elements with `inline-flex` — a small correctness detail that, if ignored, produces hydration warnings and subtly invalid markup that works until it doesn't. It's a good microcosm of the whole build: the memorable part is the wax seal and the dashed rail, but the part that makes it *ship* is remembering that a button can't contain a div. Both matter, and only one of them is fun to talk about.

## Article 6 — The Slider That Can Weaken a Firewall: Designing for Consequential Settings

Most settings pages are low-stakes. Toggle dark mode, change a default sort, pick a timezone — get it wrong and nothing burns. Echelon's threshold editor is not that kind of settings page. Every slider on it directly controls what the firewall lets through, and a careless drag can silently reopen the door to an attack the system was previously catching. When a control is genuinely consequential, the design job changes: you are no longer just exposing a value, you are helping someone *understand the consequence of changing it before they commit*. That reframing produced the two decisions I care most about in this phase.

The first is that every threshold slider sits on top of a **live histogram of where recent traffic actually scored** at that layer, split into a "pass" region and a "block" region right at the threshold line. This matters because a bare slider from 0 to 1 is an abstraction — 0.60 versus 0.72 means nothing to a human without context. But when you can *see* that a fat cluster of real prompts scored between 0.65 and 0.75, you understand viscerally that nudging the threshold from 0.60 to 0.75 isn't a small tweak; it's the difference between catching that cluster and waving it through. The histogram turns an abstract number into a decision with visible stakes, and it costs almost nothing — the score data already flows through the app for the audit log. Good information design is often just *showing the operator the data they're implicitly reasoning about anyway*.

The second decision is the **what-if guardrail**, and here the interesting design question was: how paternalistic should a security tool be? I considered three postures — informational-only (show the impact, never nag), a hard block (disable Save on any regression), and a middle path. I landed on warn-plus-confirm, because the extremes both fail a real user. Informational-only is negligent for a safety product; a hard block insults an expert who may have a legitimate reason to accept a regression. So when a pending change would free a previously-blocked known attack, the console replays recent traffic, counts the regressions, *names the attack categories* ("including prompt injection, jailbreak"), and turns the Save button into a deliberate two-step: "I understand, save anyway." The friction is proportional to the risk — zero friction for a safe change, one honest speed-bump for a dangerous one. And because this logic is the one part of the whole frontend where a bug could actively make a user *less* safe, I didn't trust it to "looks right in the UI": I pulled the pure functions out and unit-checked the regression math directly — raising a threshold frees attacks, lowering doesn't, disabling a layer frees attacks, no change is zero. The rule I'd generalize from this phase: when a setting has real consequences, spend your effort on the *consequence-preview*, not the control, and never let the safety-critical arithmetic live only inside a component you eyeballed once.

## Article 7 — Secrets, Destruction, and the Ethics of a Confirm Button

The Access Management module looks, at a glance, like the least interesting screen in the console — it's a list of API keys. But it's where a frontend's relationship with *irreversibility* gets tested, and irreversibility is a design problem disguised as a feature list. Two moments on this screen can't be taken back: creating a key (whose secret exists in plaintext exactly once) and revoking one (which instantly breaks every application still using it). How the UI handles those two moments says more about whether you can trust the tool than any amount of visual polish.

For key creation I used the **reveal-once** pattern that Stripe and GitHub have made the industry norm, and it's worth being explicit about *why* it's the right default rather than just cargo-culting it. A secret you can re-read from a dashboard is a secret sitting in a database in a form someone can exfiltrate; showing it exactly once, at creation, and storing only the last four characters afterward means a database breach leaks nothing usable. The frontend's job is to make that one-time moment unmissable: the new secret drops into a bordered banner in the brand color, with a copy button and an unambiguous warning — "copy this now, Echelon won't show it again." The interesting restraint was choosing the *inline* variant over a modal. A modal screams for attention but also traps you; an inline banner that persists at the top of the list until you dismiss it respects that you might want to look at the key alongside the rest of your keys while you wire it up. Same safety guarantee, less theater. For revocation — the destructive twin — the rule is simply that no irreversible action is ever one click. The Revoke button reveals an inline confirmation that states the actual consequence in plain language ("any application still using it will be rejected immediately") before a second, deliberate click. This is the same philosophy as the threshold guardrail two phases back: *friction proportional to consequence.* A safe action is instant; a dangerous one earns one honest speed-bump, phrased as what happens, not "are you sure?".

Stepping back, this closes out the four core modules, and a pattern has held across all of them that's worth naming as the through-line of the whole build. Every screen was designed to serve two people at once through progressive disclosure — the PM reads the top, the engineer drills to the bottom — and every screen keeps its most consequential machinery honest: colors validated by a script, not an eye; a signature component whose geometry tells the truth about how the firewall works; safety-critical arithmetic pulled out and unit-tested; irreversible actions gated behind plain-language confirmation. None of that is visible in a screenshot, which is exactly the point. The visible layer — the assay-certificate aesthetic, the cool paper, the engraved serif — is what makes the product feel considered. The invisible layer — the validated palette, the seams that let a mock become a real API without touching a component, the confirmations — is what makes it trustworthy. A security console has to be both, because a tool that guards against attacks cannot itself be the weakest, most careless thing in the room.

## Article 8 — Polish Is a Feature: Live-Tail, ⌘K, and the Flash of the Wrong Theme

There's a temptation, once the core modules work, to call a project done. But the difference between software that *works* and software that feels *finished* lives almost entirely in the last layer — the live updates, the keyboard shortcuts, the absence of a jarring flash on load. This phase was about that layer, and each piece taught the same lesson from a different angle: polish is not decoration, it's the removal of small frictions that each individually seem ignorable and collectively decide whether people trust the tool.

Take the **flash of the wrong theme**. If you persist a user's dark-mode choice in localStorage and apply it in a React effect, there's a window — a few hundred milliseconds after the HTML paints but before hydration — where the page shows light mode and then snaps to dark. It's brief, but it reads as *broken*, and on a security console that's an especially bad first impression. The fix is a tiny synchronous script injected into the document head that reads localStorage and sets the theme attribute *before the browser paints a single pixel*. It's four lines of code that most of the app never thinks about, and it's the difference between "considered" and "janky." The **live-tail** feature had a subtler version of the same trap. Streaming new rows into a virtualized table sounds simple until you remember that virtualization mounts and unmounts row elements as you scroll — so if you highlight a "new" row with a CSS mount animation, every fresh row re-flashes its arrival animation every time it scrolls back into view. The right model is to track freshness as *state with a timer* (an id is "fresh" for 2.6 seconds, then it isn't) and render it as a static style, so the highlight is truthful — it marks genuinely recent arrivals — and never lies by re-animating old data. And critically, I built live-tail as a hook with a return shape identical to what a real WebSocket subscription would produce, so the simulated stream is a stand-in, not a throwaway: when the backend ships, one hook body changes and the UI doesn't notice.

The **command palette** is the piece that most separates a tool power-users love from one they merely tolerate. ⌘K is now a genre convention, and meeting that convention is itself a form of respect — an engineer who lives in Linear and GitHub expects to be able to summon navigation without reaching for the mouse, and a security analyst chasing an incident at 2 a.m. wants to jump between the audit log and the threshold config in one keystroke. The engineering is unglamorous (a global keydown listener, arrow-key navigation, a filtered list) but the discipline is in restraint: I limited the palette to commands that genuinely exist and genuinely help — navigate, toggle theme — rather than padding it with hypothetical actions the mock backend can't actually perform. An honest, small palette beats an impressive, half-working one. That's really the summary of this whole build. Six phases in, the console has a validated palette, a signature component whose geometry tells the truth, safety-critical logic that's been unit-tested, irreversible actions behind plain-language confirmation, and now a polish layer that removes the small lies — the theme flash, the re-animating row — that erode trust. The last mile, wiring the real Echelon API, is deliberately the least dramatic step, because every module was built against a typed seam designed to make that swap boring. Boring, at the integration boundary, is the highest compliment a frontend architecture can earn.

## Article 9 — What to Test When You Can't Test Everything

With the modules built and polished, the temptation is to write tests for *everything* — snapshot every component, click every button in an E2E harness — and end up with a slow, brittle suite nobody trusts. I took the opposite approach and asked a sharper question: on this specific product, where would a bug actually hurt someone? The answer wasn't the charts or the drawer animations. It was three places, and those are the only three I wrote unit tests for. First, the **what-if guardrail** — the arithmetic that decides whether a threshold change is silently reopening the door to a known attack. A false negative there doesn't crash the app; it makes a security engineer *believe they're protected when they aren't*, which is the worst possible failure for a firewall console. So that logic gets the most tests: raising a threshold must count regressions, lowering must not, disabling a layer must free the attacks it was catching, a freed *clean* prompt must never be miscounted as a freed attack. Second, the **log filters**, because an analyst hunting an incident who gets silently-wrong filter results will draw wrong conclusions. Third, the **formatters**, because "1.86 ms" versus "1860 µs" versus "0.0 ms" is the difference between a latency figure that tells the truth and one that lies by rounding.

The discipline that made this tractable is that all three live in pure functions with no React, no DOM, no network — `computeImpact(events, saved, pending)`, `applyFilters(events, filters)`, `formatLatency(us)`. That's not an accident; it's the payoff of a decision made back in Phase 2, when I put filtering behind a plain function instead of tangling it into a component's render. Pure functions are the cheapest things in software to test exhaustively, so the architecture that separates logic from presentation is also the architecture that's cheap to verify. The suite runs in 130 milliseconds and covers the exact places where correctness is load-bearing, and I deliberately did *not* write render tests for the components — those are covered adequately by the type system, the build, and visual QA, and adding them would trade real speed for marginal confidence.

The rest of the hardening pass was the unglamorous hygiene that separates a demo from something you'd actually deploy: driving the dependency audit to zero vulnerabilities (which required pinning patched transitive versions via `overrides`, because the naive `audit fix` wanted to downgrade the framework by six major versions), and closing the accessibility gap by trapping keyboard focus inside the modal surfaces so a keyboard-only user can't tab their way out of an open dialog into the page behind it. None of this shows up in a screenshot. All of it shows up the first time a real user with a screen reader, or a security team with a compliance checklist, or an engineer reading the audit report, decides whether this tool is serious. That's been the quiet thesis of the whole build, stated one final way: a security console earns trust in the layers you can't see — the validated palette, the tested arithmetic, the trapped focus, the zeroed audit — at least as much as in the assay-certificate aesthetic you can. Both are the job. Neither is optional.
